<i>Reading time: about 8 minutes · 19 November 2026 · Science Translational Medicine, 2026</i>

After a spinal cord injury, the hand may still exist, but its touch signals may no longer reach the brain. This paper follows five people who had tiny electrodes placed in the brain’s touch area and asks, over years of use, what happened to their safety, and whether those electrodes could still evoke a sensation of touch.

## Background

A spinal cord injury (SCI) can interrupt the pathways that carry movement commands down from the brain and sensory signals back up from the body. Below the level of injury, people may lose voluntary movement and also lose or diminish the sense of touch. For people with tetraplegia — impairment of the arms and legs — restoring more useful hand and arm function is often a primary rehabilitation goal.

An <b>implantable brain–computer interface</b> (iBCI) tries to bypass the damaged pathway. Most motor iBCIs record movement-related activity from the cortex and use it to control a cursor, a robotic arm, or stimulation of muscles. Those systems often still lack the touch feedback that a native hand uses to judge contact and force.

One way to restore a form of touch is <b>intracortical microstimulation</b> (ICMS): brief, very small electrical pulses delivered through microelectrodes implanted in the <b>somatosensory cortex</b>, the cortical region that represents bodily sensation. The part studied here is Brodmann’s <b>area 1</b>, in the hand representation. A pulse train through one electrode can evoke a tactile percept that the person locates on a particular patch of skin. That patch is the electrode’s <b>projected field</b>. When ICMS is combined with a motor iBCI, those percepts can be used as artificial tactile feedback and can improve hand and arm performance.

For such a system to become a clinical device, two questions have to be answered over long periods, not just over weeks. First, what happens to the person: are there <b>serious adverse events</b> — events that are life-threatening, require hospitalization, or cause lasting disability — and are there other, less severe <b>adverse events</b> linked to stimulation? Second, what happens to the sensations and to the electrodes: does the current needed to evoke a feeling rise, do channels stop working, and do the quality and location of the percepts stay usable?

Those questions are easy to mix together. The number of years a device has been in the body is not the same as the number of hours it has actually been stimulating. A count of functional electrodes is not the same as a detection threshold, and neither is the same as whether the evoked sensations still cover the hand or still feel like the same kind of touch. This paper reports those quantities separately.

## Problem

Human evidence on the long-term safety and usefulness of ICMS had been limited. Earlier safety observations were much shorter, and reports of human ICMS lasting a few years did not amount to a systematic, multi-person safety and longevity analysis. Animal work had already set conservative stimulation limits, including studies in which earlier, more aggressive protocols could damage tissue near the electrode tip, and later primate work with refined parameters that was used to define the limits for this trial.

The authors therefore looked back across an ongoing early-feasibility trial and asked whether ICMS in human somatosensory cortex, delivered within those limits, remained clinically acceptable and still evoked informative tactile percepts as implants aged — in one person for about a decade, and in others for a few years.

## Method

### How the stimulation was delivered

Each participant received two Blackrock NeuroPort microelectrode arrays in the hand representation of area 1, and two more arrays in the hand and arm region of motor cortex, all contralateral to the dominant (right) hand (Fig. 1A). The motor arrays were used for recording and as an unstimulated comparison when electrode-health metrics were tracked. ICMS itself was delivered to the sensory arrays with a CereStim R96 stimulator.

Each sensory array is a 6-by-10 grid with 400 μm spacing and 1.5 mm shanks coated in sputtered iridium oxide. Thirty-two electrodes on each array were available for stimulation, so each person had 64 stimulation channels. Pulses were current-controlled, biphasic, and charge-balanced: a 200 μs cathodic phase, a 100 μs interphase, and a 400 μs half-amplitude anodic phase. The maximum frequency and amplitude used in the trial, 300 Hz and 100 μA (20 nC per phase; about 0.5 mC/cm² assuming a 4000 μm² tip), were taken from prior animal studies that motivated the clinical protocol.

A <b>detection threshold</b> is the current at which a person can reliably tell that a pulse train is present. In this paper, an electrode was called <b>functional</b> when its median detection threshold in a given time window stayed below the 100 μA ceiling — that is, when it could still evoke a sensation without exceeding the trial’s amplitude limit. Threshold, functional count, projected-field coverage, and reported quality were analyzed as separate measures.

## Experiments

The data come from a retrospective analysis of the early-feasibility trial NCT01894802, conducted at the University of Pittsburgh and the University of Chicago under a U.S. FDA Investigational Device Exemption. Safety data were reviewed biannually by an independent data and safety monitoring board. Five men with cervical SCI were implanted between 2015 and 2023. All were older than 25 and right-handed, and all remained implanted at the time of writing.

Their injuries and residual sensation differed. C1 (C4, AIS D) and C2 (C4, AIS D, plus a right brachial plexopathy) had some remaining hand sensation, though C2 was nearly insensate. P2 (C5 motor / C6 sensory, AIS B) and P3 (C6, AIS B) were insensate on the ulnar side of the hand but retained diminished sensation on the thumb and index. P4 (C4, AIS A) had no spared sensation at or below the C5 dermatome.

A central design choice in how the time is counted is to keep <b>individual follow-up</b> separate from <b>cumulative implant duration</b> (person-years). At the time of writing, the five people had been implanted for 33, 34, 63, 65, and 129 months. Those intervals add up to 324 months, or 27 years of combined implant time. They do not mean that any one person was followed for 27 years, and they are not 27 years of continuous stimulation.

Actual stimulation was session-based. Across participants there were 225 to 1390 experimental sessions, and 37% to 74% of those sessions included ICMS, for a combined 623 hours of stimulation, 173 million pulses, and 1724 mC of charge (Table 1 of the paper). Pulse and charge counts also varied widely from person to person:

Safety was scored in several layers. The trial’s primary safety criterion was that the device would not have to be removed for a safety concern in the first year. The paper also reports surgical and implant-hardware events, <b>ICMS-related adverse events</b>, and, in the four participants who retained some natural hand sensation, monofilament thresholds before implantation and at the time of writing. A persistent sensation — a feeling that continued after the pulse train ended — was logged as an adverse event even when it was brief and required no treatment, because it is unintended and could, in a feedback system, be mistaken for contact.

Efficacy and longevity were tracked with regular single-electrode detection thresholds (118 to 2032 measurements per person; first sensations appeared 14 to 111 days after implant), the proportion of functional electrodes, surveys of projected-field location and quality, and electrode-health recordings: signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), peak-to-peak voltage, 1 kHz impedance, and interphase voltage. Those recording metrics were compared between the stimulated sensory arrays and the unstimulated motor arrays, and against the charge delivered to each electrode.

## Results

### Surgical events and ICMS-related events

The implantations were described as well tolerated, with no major intraoperative or postoperative complications, and no infection, intracranial hemorrhage, neurological deterioration, or new-onset seizures attributed to the surgery. The first-year explant-for-safety criterion was met in all five people. Two participants later needed a minor revision to resecure a loose percutaneous pedestal; one of those also had minimal skin debridement at the pedestal site. These events were not accompanied by neurological sequelae, systemic infection, or electrode malfunction.

No serious adverse events were associated with ICMS. There were, however, 53 ICMS-related adverse events, all of them persistent sensations (3, 3, 25, 16, and 6 events in C1, C2, P2, P3, and P4). The sensations stayed in the original projected field, were never described as painful, required no medical intervention, and had no lasting effects. Most lasted less than 10 seconds; 36% lasted 10 to 60 seconds; one event in P3 lasted between 2 and 10 minutes, and the longest reported sensation was about 9.5 minutes. On average there was one event per 23,000 stimulation trials, but the rate varied substantially (from about one per 3,000 trials in P4 to one per 79,000 in C1). For three of the five people, events were more common after multielectrode than single-electrode stimulation. Participants with SCI can also have spontaneous residual sensations, and they were asked to report a persistent sensation even when the two were hard to tell apart, so the count may include some false positives.

In the four participants who still had at least partial natural touch in the same parts of the hand, monofilament thresholds did not decrease between the pre-implant test and the time of writing.

### Thresholds, functional electrodes, and percepts

Initial detection thresholds were low in four people (median 14.5 to 22.5 μA) and higher in C2 (median 61.5 μA), who also had the longest delay to first sensation. Across electrodes, thresholds rose by an average of 3.67 ± 11.1 μA per year, with significant differences among participants; P4 did not have enough measurements for a slope (Fig. 2, A and B). The abstract rounds this average to about 3.5 μA per year. There was no consistent relationship between the charge delivered to an electrode and its change in threshold.

The functional-electrode count is a different number. One year after implant, more than 75% of electrodes were functional in C1, P2, and P3 (Fig. 2C). At four years, 80% remained functional in C1 and 75% in P3. At 10 years, 60% remained functional in P2, the longest-implanted participant. At the most recent time point, 56% of P4’s electrodes and 48% of C2’s were functional; P4’s thresholds had risen more quickly after the first year, whereas C2’s initially high thresholds later showed improved sensitivity. Across the five people, the most recent proportion of functional electrodes was 64 ± 13%, described in the paper as about a 21% decrease.

Even as some electrodes crossed the 100 μA limit, somatotopic coverage of the hand remained largely unchanged, because projected fields overlapped (Fig. 2D). Quality reports — mostly mechanical (pressure, touch) or paresthetic (tingle, electrical), with some vibration or buzzing — were consistent over time for C1, P2, and P3 (Fig. 2E). Rated naturalness was stable or increased, except in P2, where it began to fall after the eighth year. Two participants regularly reported mild ICMS-associated pain, but those ratings were well below their baseline pain from the injury (median 1.5 versus 7).

### Electrode-health recordings

SNR and peak-to-peak voltage tended to decline on both sensory and motor arrays (median SNR slopes from −0.28 to −0.02 per year). The comparison between stimulated and unstimulated arrays was mixed: in one person sensory SNR fell faster, in another motor SNR fell faster, and in the others the difference was not significant. For peak-to-peak voltage, sensory electrodes declined more slowly than motor electrodes in three people. Impedance decayed exponentially on all arrays, without a consistent sensory-versus-motor difference. Interphase voltages drifted in different directions across participants. None of these recording metrics, nor the charge delivered, was consistently related to whether an electrode remained detectable.

## Analysis

The study’s main contribution is to place several long-term measurements side by side in the same five people, rather than to collapse them into a single safety or success rate.

On safety, the trial observed no serious adverse events linked to ICMS, and no decrease in residual monofilament sensitivity in the four people who still had natural touch in the stimulated skin regions. It also recorded 53 non-serious, ICMS-related persistent sensations, and two later pedestal revisions with one site debridement. Those facts describe the clinical events that were logged under this protocol. Safety here was assessed with event reports, residual sensation tests, and electrode recordings, during laboratory sessions that amounted to 623 hours of stimulation — on the order of tens of minutes per session, several times a week — not with direct inspection of the tissue under the arrays.

On efficacy, a slowly rising average threshold, a falling share of functional electrodes, and relatively stable projected-field coverage and quality can all be true at once. The 64 ± 13% figure is the most recent functional proportion across the five participants. The 60% figure is P2’s proportion after 10 years. C2 and P4, followed for a shorter time, ended near 48% and 56%. The 27 years in the paper are the sum of five implant intervals; the individual stories run from just under three years to a little more than ten.

The recording comparisons suggest that many changes at the electrode–tissue interface tracked implant duration more closely than the amount of ICMS delivered. In that sense, the longevity of the sensations is a property of a chronic implant used at these parameters, not only a property of the pulse count.

## Summary

In five people with cervical spinal cord injury, ICMS of the hand area of somatosensory cortex was followed for 33 to 129 months — 27 years of combined implant time — and 173 million pulses. No serious ICMS-related adverse events were recorded; 53 brief persistent sensations were. At the most recent measurements, 64 ± 13% of electrodes still evoked tactile percepts, including 60% after 10 years in one participant, while average detection thresholds rose slowly and the location and quality of the sensations remained largely consistent. Those are separate results, and together they describe what this trial measured over years of laboratory use.

脊髓损伤之后，手还在，但触觉信号未必还能传到大脑。这篇论文随访了五名在大脑触觉区植入微电极的患者，问的是：经过多年使用，安全性如何，这些电极是否仍能引出触觉。

## 背景

脊髓损伤会打断大脑下行的运动指令，也会打断身体上行的感觉信号。在损伤平面以下，人可能失去主动运动，触觉也会减弱或消失。对于四肢功能都受影响的患者来说，让手和胳膊重新变得更好用，往往是最优先的康复目标。

<b>植入式脑机接口</b>（iBCI）试图绕过这段受损通路。多数运动脑机接口从皮层记录与运动有关的活动，用来控制光标、机械臂或肌肉刺激。这些系统常常仍然缺少一只完整的手所依赖的触觉反馈。

恢复某种触觉的一条路径，是<b>皮层内微刺激</b>（intracortical microstimulation, ICMS）：通过植入<b>体感皮层</b>的微电极，施加非常短暂、幅度很小的电脉冲。体感皮层是表征身体感觉的皮层区域。本文关注的是其中 Brodmann <b>1 区</b>的手部表征。经过一根电极的脉冲串，可以引出定位在某一小块皮肤上的触觉；这块皮肤就是该电极的<b>投射野</b>。如果把 ICMS 与运动脑机接口结合，这些感知可以用作人工触觉反馈，并改善手和手臂的操作。

要让这样的系统走向临床，需要在很长的时间里分别回答两类问题。第一，人怎么样：有没有<b>严重不良事件</b>——危及生命、需要住院或造成持久功能损害的事件——以及与刺激相关的其他、较轻的<b>不良事件</b>？第二，感觉和电极怎么样：引出感觉所需的电流会不会升高，通道会不会失效，感知的性质和位置是否仍然可用？

这些问题不宜混成一个数字。装置留在体内的年数，并不等于实际施加刺激的小时数；仍能工作的电极数量，也不等于检测阈，更不等于投射野是否还覆盖手、感觉是否还是同一种性质。这篇论文把这些量分开报告。

## 问题

关于人类 ICMS 长期安全性和可用性的证据原先比较有限。更早的安全性观察时间要短得多，也有一些人类 ICMS 使用数年的报告，但还缺少系统的、覆盖多人的安全与寿命分析。动物实验已经给出了偏保守的刺激限制：较早、更强的方案曾在电极尖端附近造成组织损伤，后来在非人灵长类中用更精细的参数完成的研究，则被用来设定本试验的限度。

因此，作者回顾一项正在进行的早期可行性试验，问的是：在这些限度之内，对人类体感皮层施加 ICMS，随着植入物老化——其中一人约十年，其他人几年——在临床上是否仍然可接受，以及是否还能引出有信息量的触觉。

## 方法

### 刺激是怎样施加的

每名参与者在 1 区手部表征植入两块 Blackrock NeuroPort 微电极阵列，并在运动皮层的手和臂区再植入两块，均位于优势手（右手）的对侧（图 1A）。运动皮层阵列用于记录，并在追踪电极健康指标时作为未受刺激的对照。ICMS 本身由 CereStim R96 刺激器送到体感阵列。

每块体感阵列是 6×10 的电极栅，间距 400 μm，电极柄长 1.5 mm，表面镀有溅射氧化铱。每块阵列有 32 个电极可用于刺激，因此每人有 64 个刺激通道。脉冲为电流控制、双相、电荷平衡：200 μs 阴极相，100 μs 相间隔，再接 400 μs、幅度减半的阳极相。试验中使用的最高频率和幅度是 300 Hz 和 100 μA（每相 20 nC；若按尖端面积 4000 μm² 计算，电荷密度约 0.5 mC/cm²），来自此前推动本试验的动物研究。

<b>检测阈</b>是指人能够稳定判断脉冲串存在时的电流。本文把电极称为<b>有效</b>（functional），是指在给定时间窗内，其中位检测阈仍低于 100 μA 的上限——也就是不必超过试验允许的最大电流，仍能引出感觉。检测阈、有效电极数量、投射野覆盖和报告的感觉性质，是分开分析的指标。

## 实验

数据来自对早期可行性试验 NCT01894802 的回顾分析，试验在匹兹堡大学和芝加哥大学进行，并获得美国 FDA 的试验器械豁免。安全性数据由独立的数据和安全监查委员会每半年审阅一次。2015 至 2023 年间，五名颈髓损伤的男性接受了植入。他们植入时均大于 25 岁，皆为右利手，撰写论文时装置仍都在体内。

损伤和残存感觉并不相同。C1（C4，AIS D）和 C2（C4，AIS D，并伴右侧臂丛损伤）仍有部分手部感觉，但 C2 几乎没有。P2（运动 C5 / 感觉 C6，AIS B）和 P3（C6，AIS B）在手尺侧没有感觉，但拇指和食指仍有减弱的感觉。P4（C4，AIS A）在 C5 皮节及以下没有残留感觉。

计时上一个关键区别，是把<b>个体随访时间</b>和<b>累计植入时间</b>（人年）分开。撰写时，五人分别已植入 33、34、63、65 和 129 个月。这些时间加起来是 324 个月，也就是合计 27 年的植入时间。这并不表示任何一个人被随访了 27 年，更不是连续刺激了 27 年。

实际刺激是按实验场次进行的。五人分别完成 225 到 1390 次实验，其中 37% 到 74% 的场次包含 ICMS，合计刺激 623 小时、1.73 亿个脉冲、1724 mC 电荷（见原论文 Table 1）。每个人的脉冲数和电荷量也相差很大：

| C1 | 63 | 325.9 | 69.1 | 3 |
| C2 | 33 | 50.1 | 38.2 | 3 |

| P3 | 65 | 65.7 | 11.8 | 16 |

安全性从几层来记录。试验的主要安全性标准，是第一年内不必因安全原因取出装置。论文还报告了手术和植入硬件事件、<b>与 ICMS 相关的不良事件</b>，以及在四名仍保留部分手部自然感觉的参与者中，植入前和撰写时的单丝触觉阈。刺激结束后仍然持续的感觉被称为持续感觉；即便短暂、也不需要治疗，仍被记为不良事件，因为它并非预期效应，在反馈系统中还可能被误当成接触。

有效性和寿命则通过定期的单电极检测阈（每人 118 到 2032 次测量；首次引出感觉出现在植入后 14 到 111 天）、有效电极比例、投射野位置与性质的问卷，以及电极健康记录来追踪：包括信噪比（SNR）、峰峰值电压、1 kHz 阻抗和相间电压。这些记录指标会在受到刺激的体感阵列和未受刺激的运动阵列之间比较，并与每根电极输送的电荷对照。

## 结果

### 手术事件和与 ICMS 相关的事件

植入过程被描述为耐受良好，没有重大的术中或术后并发症，也没有归因于手术的感染、颅内出血、神经功能恶化和新发癫痫。五人모두满足“第一年不必因安全原因取出装置”这一标准。后来有两人因经皮基座松动做了小的翻修，其中一人还在基座部位做了很小的皮肤清创。这些事件没有伴随神经后遗症、全身感染或电极故障。

没有与 ICMS 相关的严重不良事件。但有 53 例与 ICMS 相关的不良事件，全部是持续感觉（C1、C2、P2、P3、P4 分别为 3、3、25、16 和 6 次）。这些感觉仍留在原来的投射野内，从未被描述为疼痛，不需要医疗干预，也没有持久影响。大多数持续不到 10 秒；36% 持续 10 到 60 秒；P3 有一次持续 2 到 10 分钟，报告过的最长时间约为 9.5 分钟。平均每 23,000 次刺激试验出现 1 次，但个体差异很大（P4 大约每 3,000 次一次，C1 大约每 79,000 次一次）。五人中有三人在多电极刺激后比单电极刺激后更常见。脊髓损伤本身也可能带来自发的残存感觉，研究者要求在两者难以区分时也报告持续感觉，因此计数里可能包含一些假阳性。

在四名同一手区仍保留至少部分自然触觉的参与者中，植入前到撰写时的单丝阈值没有下降。

### 检测阈、有效电极和感知

四人最初的检测阈较低（中位数 14.5 到 22.5 μA），C2 较高（中位数 61.5 μA），首次引出感觉也最晚。就电极而言，检测阈平均每年升高 3.67 ± 11.1 μA，参与者之间差异显著；P4 的测量次数不足以估计斜率（图 2A、B）。摘要把这一平均值约写成每年 3.5 μA。输送到电极上的电荷，与阈值变化之间没有一致关系。

有效电极数量是另一个数字。植入一年后，C1、P2 和 P3 的有效电极都超过 75%（图 2C）。四年时，C1 仍有 80%，P3 仍有 75%。十年时，随访最长的 P2 仍有 60%。在最近一次时间点，P4 为 56%，C2 为 48%；P4 在第一年之后阈值上升更快，而 C2 起初较高的阈值后来敏感性有所改善。五人最近一次的有效电极比例是 64 ± 13%，论文中表述为大约下降了 21%。

即便部分电极越过了 100 μA 的上限，由于投射野相互重叠，手上的躯体拓扑覆盖大体保持不变（图 2D）。性质报告——主要是机械性（压、触）或感觉异常性（刺麻、电刺激感），也有振动或嗡嗡感——在 C1、P2 和 P3 中随时间保持一致（图 2E）。自然度评分保持稳定或有所提高，只有 P2 在第 8 年之后开始下降。有两名参与者经常报告与 ICMS 相关的轻微疼痛，但这些评分明显低于他们因损伤而来的基线疼痛（中位数 1.5 对 7）。

### 电极健康记录

SNR 和峰峰值电压在体感和运动阵列上往往都下降（SNR 斜率的中位数为每年 −0.28 到 −0.02）。受刺激与未受刺激阵列的比较并不一律：有一人体感 SNR 下降更快，另一人运动 SNR 下降更快，其余人差异不显著。就峰峰值电压而言，三人的体感电极比运动电极下降更慢。所有阵列的阻抗都呈指数衰减，体感与运动之间没有一致差别。相间电压在不同参与者中朝不同方向漂移。这些记录指标，以及输送的电荷，都与电极是否仍能被检测到没有一致关系。

## 分析

这项研究的主要贡献，是在同一批五名参与者身上把多项长期测量并列起来，而不是把它们压成一个安全率或成功率。

在安全性上，试验没有观察到与 ICMS 相关的严重不良事件；在四名受刺激皮肤区仍有自然触觉的人中，残存单丝敏感性也没有下降。与此同时，记录到 53 次非严重、与 ICMS 相关的持续感觉，以及后来两次基座翻修和一次局部清创。这些是该方案下登记到的临床事件。这里的安全性评估，依据的是事件报告、残存感觉测试和电极记录，刺激合计 623 小时，大约是每次实验数十分钟、每周数次的实验室使用，而不是直接检查阵列下方的组织。

在有效性上，平均检测阈缓慢上升、有效电极比例下降，与投射野覆盖和感觉性质相对稳定，可以同时成立。64 ± 13% 是五人最近一次的有效比例；60% 是 P2 在十年时的比例。随访较短的 C2 和 P4，最近分别接近 48% 和 56%。论文中的 27 年是五段植入时间之和；每个人的故事从不到三年到十年出头不等。

记录指标的比较提示，电极–组织界面上的许多变化，与植入时间的关系比与 ICMS 输送量的关系更密切。在这个意义上，感觉能够维持多久，是在这些参数下使用一套长期植入装置的结果，而不仅仅是脉冲总数的结果。

## 总结

在五名颈髓损伤患者中，对体感皮层手区进行 ICMS 的随访时间为 33 到 129 个月——合计 27 年植入时间——并施加了 1.73 亿个脉冲。没有记录到与 ICMS 相关的严重不良事件；有 53 次短暂的持续感觉。在最近一次测量中，64 ± 13% 的电极仍能引出触觉，其中一人在十年后仍有 60%；平均检测阈缓慢上升，感觉的位置和性质大体保持一致。这些是需要分开看待的结果，合在一起，描述的是这项试验在多年实验室使用中实际测到的情况。
